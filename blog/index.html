What to expect at your first hearing test

Updated 2026 • 6–10 minute read

If you have noticed conversations sounding muffled, family saying the TV is too loud, or more effort needed to follow group discussions, a hearing evaluation is the best first step. At Coastal Hearing Care, your visit is comfortable, unrushed, and designed to give you practical answers you can use right away.

How to prepare

  • Bring a list of medications and any prior hearing test results.
  • Think about specific listening situations you find challenging.
  • Bring a support person if you would like a second set of ears for the discussion.

Step-by-step visit

1) Conversation and case history. We start by learning about your hearing, health history, and lifestyle goals so testing focuses on what matters to you.

2) Otoscopy (a look in the ears). We examine your ear canals and eardrums to check for earwax, irritation, or medical concerns. If earwax is blocking the canal, we can discuss safe removal options.

3) Middle-ear check. Quick, painless measurements evaluate eardrum movement and pressure to rule out congestion or fluid issues that can affect hearing.

4) Pure-tone thresholds. You will wear headphones and press a button when you hear beeps at different pitches and volumes. This maps your hearing sensitivity for each ear.

5) Speech understanding. We measure how clearly you recognize words in quiet and, when appropriate, with background noise to understand real-world listening.

6) Results and personalized recommendations. We review your audiogram in plain language, answer questions, and outline next steps—whether monitoring, medical follow-up, communication strategies, or hearing technology.

What to bring home

  • Your audiogram and a clear explanation of what it means.
  • Customized recommendations aligned with your lifestyle and budget.
  • Practical tips to improve communication today.

Most people say they feel relief and clarity after their evaluation. If hearing aids are appropriate, we will demonstrate options and explain what to expect during a trial.

Best Practice

Why real-ear measurement matters

Updated 2026 • 5–7 minute read

Two people can wear the same hearing aid but hear very different results. That is because your ear canal shape and acoustics are unique. Real-ear measurement (REM)—also called probe microphone verification—measures sound where it matters most: right at your eardrum.

What is real-ear measurement?

REM uses a tiny, flexible microphone placed in the ear canal alongside the hearing aid. We play calibrated speech-like sounds and measure the amplified sound in your ear. Then we adjust your hearing aid until the measured sound matches prescriptive targets for your hearing loss.

Why it is essential

  • Accuracy: Programming based on in-ear measurements is more precise than using averages or manufacturer defaults.
  • Comfort and clarity: Hitting targets improves speech understanding while keeping loud sounds comfortable.
  • Confidence: You leave knowing your devices are verified to your prescription, not just “close enough.”

What the appointment feels like

The process is quick and gentle. You will hear recorded speech while we make small adjustments. Many patients notice immediate improvements in clarity and balance.

Not all clinics provide REM, but it is considered a best practice standard. We include verification in every fitting and major follow-up.

Brain & Hearing

Hearing loss and dementia: the connection

Updated 2026 • 6–8 minute read

Untreated hearing loss can make the brain work harder to follow speech, especially in noise. Over time, this extra effort may contribute to fatigue, social withdrawal, and reduced stimulation—factors that are linked in the research literature to cognitive decline.

What the science suggests

  • Hearing loss is associated with increased risk of cognitive challenges in older adults.
  • Addressing hearing loss can improve communication, social engagement, and quality of life—important for overall brain health.
  • Early identification and management are key; you do not need to “wait until it is bad.”

Practical steps you can take

  • Get a baseline hearing test by age 50 (earlier if you notice changes or have risk factors).
  • Use well-fit hearing aids when recommended and wear them consistently.
  • Stay socially active and keep your brain engaged with meaningful activities.
  • Prioritize sleep, exercise, and chronic condition management in partnership with your healthcare team.

While no single intervention prevents dementia, supporting healthy hearing is a proactive step you can control. We are here to guide you with clear information and individualized care.

Tinnitus

Tinnitus relief: what actually works

Updated 2026 • 7–9 minute read

Tinnitus—the perception of ringing, buzzing, or hissing without an external source—is common and often manageable. The best approach combines education, sound therapy, and support tailored to your needs.

Evidence-backed options

  • Hearing aids for hearing loss: Restoring access to ambient sound can reduce tinnitus awareness and listening effort.
  • Sound therapy: Gentle sound (from devices, apps, or bedside options) helps the brain tune out tinnitus over time.
  • Counseling and coping skills: Education, stress reduction, and sleep strategies reduce the impact of tinnitus on daily life.

Approaches to skip

  • Ear candles and unproven “detox” remedies.
  • Overly loud masking that competes with conversation.

What to expect at our clinic

We begin with a thorough history and hearing evaluation, screen for red flags that require medical referral, and then create a plan using sound therapy tools and coaching. Most people notice gradual improvement with steady use.

Choosing your first hearing aids

Updated 2026 • 6–8 minute read

Start with your goals, not a brand

Think about where you most want help—restaurants, meetings, phone calls, or TV. Your priorities and hearing test results determine the right style and features.

Common styles

  • Receiver-in-canal (RIC): Discreet, comfortable, versatile for many hearing losses.
  • In-the-ear (ITE/ITC/CIC): Custom-fit options that sit in the ear; size affects features and battery life.
  • Behind-the-ear (BTE): Durable and powerful, often for more significant hearing losses.

Features to consider

  • Rechargeable batteries and reliable Bluetooth connectivity.
  • Directional microphones and noise management for speech in noise.
  • Telecoil and accessories for phone, TV, and public venues.
  • App controls and remote care support.

Fit and follow-up matter most

Precise programming, real-ear verification, and coaching are what turn devices into results. Expect a trial period, fine-tuning appointments, and long-term support.

Ear Health

Earwax: when to leave it alone, when to see a professional

Updated 2026 • 4–6 minute read

Earwax (cerumen) moisturizes the ear canal and traps debris. Many ears self-clean, moving wax outward naturally. Problems arise when wax gets pushed deep with cotton swabs or builds up faster than it can clear.

Do’s and don’ts

  • Do wipe the outer ear with a soft cloth after showers.
  • Do ask us before using drops if you have ear surgery history, ear tubes, or pain.
  • Don’t insert cotton swabs, hair pins, or ear candles—they can push wax deeper or cause injury.

When to seek help

  • Muffled hearing, fullness, itching, or feedback in hearing aids.
  • Pain, drainage, or sudden hearing change—these warrant prompt evaluation.

Professional removal

We use safe techniques such as illumination, curettage, or gentle irrigation when appropriate. If a medical condition is suspected, we will coordinate with your physician.
